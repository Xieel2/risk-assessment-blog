The SOC Pilot Era: Evaluating Realism, Responsibility, and Contextual Failure in Autonomous Security Operations

Introduction

Security Operations Centers (SOCs) across the globe are operating in a state of perpetual crisis. Cybersecurity teams face exponential growth in alert volumes, sophisticated multi-stage threats, and a chronic shortage of skilled security talent. In response to this operational bottleneck, the paradigm of security automation is shifting from deterministic scripts and basic SOAR playbooks toward multi-agentic autonomous systems.

In his seminal IBM article, How agentic AI enables an autonomous SOC with minimal human involvement, John Velisaris (2025) articulates a vision where agentic AI handles low-level threat detection, investigation, and routine mitigation. Velisaris compares this future SOC to a modern commercial airliner: the AI agent acts as an autopilot handling standard flight operations, while human security analysts step into the role of "SOC pilots" who intervene primarily when encountering complex, non-standard scenarios such as zero-day exploits or anomalous systemic behaviors.

However, transitioning human analysts from manual execution to supervisory steering introduces critical questions: Is the "SOC pilot" model both realistic and responsible? Where can agentic AI safely operate independently, where must human judgment remain indispensable, and what happens when the underlying asset telemetry fed into an AI agent is flawed? This paper evaluates the SOC pilot framework, examines the catastrophic risks of context-less automation, and outlines the essential skills required for cybersecurity professionals to thrive as SOC pilots rather than being rendered obsolete.

Evaluating the Realism and Responsibility of the SOC Pilot Model

Is the Aviation Metaphor Realistic?

The aviation metaphor provided by Velisaris (2025) offers a compelling vision for cybersecurity operations. Modern commercial aircraft rely on highly reliable autopilot systems to perform routine tasks—maintaining cruise altitude, managing fuel consumption, and tracking flight paths—leaving human pilots to monitor telemetry, handle unexpected atmospheric changes, and make critical decisions during emergencies.

In cybersecurity, the realism of this model relies on the rise of agentic AI—autonomous software entities that do not merely execute pre-programmed decision trees, but possess reasoning capabilities, tool-use autonomy, and the ability to dynamically adjust sub-tasks to achieve a high-level goal. For repetitive, high-frequency tasks such as correlate-and-quarantine alerts, endpoint isolation during known malware outbreaks, or automated log parsing, agentic AI operates with speed and precision far exceeding human capability. In this narrow domain, the SOC pilot model is highly realistic.

However, a fundamental discrepancy exists between aviation and cybersecurity: the environment of flight is governed by consistent physical laws, whereas the enterprise threat landscape is actively hostile, unpredictable, and constantly manipulated by human adversaries. Turbulence follows predictable meteorological fluid dynamics; an advanced persistent threat (APT) actor intentionally obfuscates actions to exploit subtle architectural weaknesses. Thus, while agentic AI can handle standard "cruise" operations, expecting AI agents to operate cleanly without human supervision assumes a static environment that does not exist in modern IT ecosystems.

Is the Model Responsible?

From a governance and risk management perspective, delegating tactical execution to AI while retaining strategic oversight with human "pilots" is a responsible approach—provided strict guardrails are enforced. Responsible autonomy demands non-repudiation, clear operational boundaries, and dynamic audit logs.

If security leaders treat agentic AI as an unmonitored replacement for human teams rather than an intelligence multiplier, the model degenerates into an irresponsible liability. Responsibility requires that human SOC pilots retain ultimate authority over destructive remediation actions, maintain continuous visibility into agentic reasoning chains, and conduct routine algorithmic audits to guard against drift and hallucinations.

Where Agentic AI Can Safely Act on Its Own

AI agents can safely operate with full autonomy in bounded, low-blast-radius environments where decisions are deterministic and operational disruption is minimal:

  • Known Threat Remediation: Isolating non-critical endpoints infected with known signature-matched ransomware families or blocking malicious external IP addresses at the perimeter firewall.
  • Triage and Contextual Enrichment: Automatically querying threat intelligence feeds, gathering process trees, parsing user behavior analytics (UBA), and synthesizing log events prior to alerting human analysts.
  • Credential Invalidation for Low-Privilege Accounts: Automatically revoking session tokens or forcing password resets for non-administrative accounts exhibiting verified credential-stuffing patterns.

Where Human Judgment Must Remain Indispensable

Human "SOC pilots" must retain absolute authority in high-stakes scenarios involving systemic business disruption, novel threat patterns, or deep context sensitivity:

  • Zero-Day Attacks and Novel TTPs: When an attack leverages previously unknown vulnerabilities or novel Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures (TTPs) that lack historical training data, probabilistic AI models fail. Human pilots possess domain intuition and cross-domain reasoning required to identify subtle anomalies.
  • Mission-Critical Asset Remediation: Severing network connectivity or locking databases hosting core financial databases, healthcare record storage, or industrial control systems (ICS). An automated isolation of a primary Active Directory controller could halt enterprise operations globally.
  • Crisis Management and Strategic Containment: Deciding whether to shut down a production line, notifying regulatory bodies (such as mandatory 72-hour notifications under data privacy laws), or managing legal liabilities during a breach event.

The Fragility of Autonomy: The Impact of Missing or Flawed Asset Telemetry

The Contextual Failure Mode

As Velisaris (2025) emphasizes, automation fails without context. An AI security agent cannot determine the appropriate risk-adjusted response to a threat alert unless it accurately knows the business criticality of the affected system.

For instance, consider a suspicious PowerShell script execution detected on a workstation:

  • If the machine is an isolated marketing laptop, the optimal response is immediate network isolation.
  • If the machine is the primary domain controller or an active surgical database server, immediate network isolation could cause catastrophic operational failure, potentially costing millions of dollars or endangering human life.

If the underlying Configuration Management Database (CMDB) or Asset Management System is outdated, incomplete, or incorrectly labeled, the AI agent will execute flawed decisions based on incorrect premises.

Risks of Flawed Asset Intelligence

  1. False Positives Resulting in Self-Inflicted Denial of Service (DoS): An AI agent misidentifying a core operational server as an ordinary employee endpoint might automatically sever its network connection upon detecting a minor anomaly, shutting down critical business operations.
  2. False Negatives and Unchecked Threat Escalation: If an agentic system misclassifies a database holding sensitive personal data as a low-value test server, it may apply low-severity triage protocols, allowing attackers unhindered access and exfiltration opportunities.
  3. Adversarial Poisoning of Asset Context: Sophisticated threat actors who gain internal access may deliberately tamper with CMDB metadata or asset tagging services to downgrade the perceived criticality of target servers, manipulating the AI agent into ignoring malicious activity on critical infrastructure.

Evolving into a SOC Pilot: Essential Skills for the Autonomous AI Era

To avoid being displaced by autonomous agentic systems, cybersecurity analysts must transition from reactive "swivel-chair" operators—who manually copy data between SIEM, EDR, and ticketing tools—into strategic SOC pilots. Building a resilient cybersecurity career alongside agentic AI requires developing technical, governance, and analytical capabilities:

  1. Multi-Agent Orchestration and AI System Engineering: Analysts must understand how agentic frameworks operate, including tool-calling mechanisms, vector database retrievals, and prompt orchestration. They must be capable of configuring, tuning, and auditing AI workflows rather than merely reading their output.
  2. Advanced Threat Hunting and Adversarial Analysis: While AI excels at detecting known patterns and processing massive data streams, human pilots must focus on threat hunting—proactively searching for covert persistence mechanisms, zero-day exploits, and subtle living-off-the-land (LotL) techniques that bypass automated detection models.
  3. AI Governance, Model Auditing, and Safety Assurance: Future security professionals must evaluate the security of AI models themselves. This includes auditing agentic decision chains for bias, drift, and hallucinations, as well as protecting internal security models from prompt injection and data poisoning attacks.
  4. Business-Centric Risk Assessment and Crisis Management: Human SOC pilots must act as the bridge between technical security telemetry and business continuity. Understanding business workflows, regulatory compliance frameworks, and organizational risk tolerance allows pilots to make nuanced decisions that balance cybersecurity defense with uninterrupted enterprise performance.

Conclusion

The transformation of the Security Operations Center through agentic AI is both an operational necessity and an inevitable evolution in modern enterprise defense. The "SOC pilot" model championed by Velisaris (2025) presents a viable path forward—one where autonomous virtual agents manage high-volume routine operations while skilled human analysts provide strategic governance, handle complex zero-day threats, and make high-stakes operational choices.

However, autonomy without accurate context is a significant liability. The failure of asset management systems can turn an AI defense mechanism into a self-inflicted threat. Ultimately, the future of security operations does not belong to fully autonomous machines, nor does it belong to overburdened manual analysts. It belongs to the skilled SOC pilots who possess the technical depth, business acumen, and governance expertise to direct agentic AI systems effectively.
